# BMW Product Manager Discussion — Groundwork

Source: [Original conversation notes](conversation-with-bmw-prod-manager.md).

This document organizes the discussion into a foundation for our next steps. The source is a set of rough notes; examples and estimates below should be read in that context.

## Core idea

Product requirements develop through an ongoing process of gathering signals, understanding their relevance, checking feasibility, and refining a vehicle-specific draft. Decisions depend on the market, customer group, vehicle class, engineering possibilities, and business constraints.

The manager emphasized that this process keeps looping. The timeline mentioned was roughly **one to two years**, with repeated reviews and deeper investigation of internal feedback.

## Where the signals come from

The discussion identified several inputs:

- **Social media:** customer feedback and requests.
- **Agencies:** another channel for input; their specific role was not detailed.
- **Competitors:** comparisons of what other vehicles offer and how quickly competitors may catch up.
- **Market contacts:** people who understand local culture, customer habits, and everyday usability.
- **Internal feedback and usage data:** deeper feedback reviews, plus data supplied through the app and use of the car.

Before prioritizing a request, the manager pointed to the number of people behind it, its sources, source reliability, the relevant region, and the functionality involved. A verification loop was also mentioned, although its exact steps were not described.

## How the work progresses

The notes suggest the following flow, with repeated loops between stages:

1. **Collect signals** from the different channels.
2. **Assess relevance and priority**, including the market and any quality issues.
3. **Speak with engineering early** to establish rough feasibility: can the requirement actually be implemented?
4. **Align the inputs and constraints** into a first draft for the specific car.
5. **Revisit the draft**, investigate internal feedback more deeply, and update requirements as new information arrives.

The initial engineering conversation is an early feasibility check. The notes do not describe a formal approval process or fixed gates between these stages.

## What shapes a decision

| Area | Questions raised in the discussion |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence | How many people are asking? Which sources support the request, and how reliable are they? |
| Market fit | Which region is relevant? Does the requirement fit local culture and how people use the car? |
| Functionality and quality | What functionality is involved? What quality issues need attention? |
| Vehicle fit | Is the request reasonable for this class of car? Does it belong in a higher class? How do vehicle size and budget affect the decision? |
| Engineering and R&D | Can it be implemented? Is it feasible from an R&D perspective? |
| Cost and business case | Is it feasible cost-wise? What are the budget, volume, and margin considerations? |
| Reuse | Can the requirement or feature be used in other vehicles? |
| Timing and flexibility | How quickly could the requirement change? How does that fit the development timeline, and can the team stay flexible? |
| Competition | How does the vehicle compare with competitors? How quickly could they catch up? |

Finding the data needed to answer these questions was itself raised as a challenge.

## Differences between markets and vehicle lines

The manager described drawing boundaries between vehicle lines as tricky. No fixed rule was given; feasibility, vehicle size, budget, customer group, and competitor comparisons all influence the decision.

The notes include illustrative customer examples: younger people in relation to the **1 Series**, and entrepreneurs in relation to the **5 or 7 Series**. These are discussion examples, not a complete segmentation model.

The discussion also referred to **“product genesis”** and the contribution of people in each market who understand its culture and basic usability needs. Their input helps assess whether a requirement fits that market.

## Timeline and when the loop ends

When asked about the timeline and criteria for ending the loop, the answer remained somewhat unspecified. The discussion raised R&D feasibility, reuse across vehicles, budget, changing requirements, functionality, competition, region, volume, and margins as relevant considerations.

The notes do not establish a fixed number of iterations, a precise stopping rule, or a final decision owner.

## Internal data collection

When asked how data is collected internally, the manager referred to the **app** and **use of the car**, through which users supply data. The notes end before this process is explained further.

The specific data collected, how it is accessed, and how it feeds into prioritization remain unclear.

## Open questions for the next stage

- What does the verification loop check, and what evidence is sufficient to prioritize a request?
- How are conflicting signals from different sources and markets resolved?
- How are requests allocated between vehicle lines in practice?
- What determines when a draft is ready and the iteration loop can end? Who makes that decision?
- Which data is available for assessing demand, feasibility, cost, volume, and margins?
- How are app and vehicle-usage data connected to customer feedback and product decisions?

These are follow-up questions derived from gaps in the notes. They are not additional requirements stated by the manager.
